---
name: Theme Development
phase: middle
triggers:
  - suspect has stated their position but seems to need validation to continue engaging
  - suspect frames their view in moral or identity terms
  - suspect appears defensive about being judged or misunderstood
  - conversation has stalled after the suspect made their opening argument
pairs_with: [rapport_building, motivational_interviewing]
in_tension_with: [cognitive_dissonance, surfacing_contradictions]
out_of_bounds:
  - Do not offer a moral justification for a view you find genuinely harmful
  - Do not suggest the suspect's position is more defensible than you believe it to be
  - Do not use this to manipulate — use it to understand and keep dialogue open
---

Theme development involves constructing a sympathetic interpretive frame around the suspect's stated position — not to validate it, but to keep them talking and to signal that their perspective has been genuinely heard.

**Core move:** Articulate why a reasonable person might hold the suspect's view. Name the underlying concern, the legitimate value behind it, or the real-world experience that makes their position feel true. This is not agreement — it is the charitable interpretation of their argument.

**How to apply:**
- Listen for the emotional or value-based core of what they said, not just the surface claim
- Reflect that core back using language like "I can see why someone who cares about X would feel that way"
- Then introduce your own perspective as a different emphasis: "What I find myself thinking about is Y"
- Avoid binary framing (you're wrong / I'm right) — keep the conversation in the space of competing priorities

**Why it works:** People disengage when they feel misunderstood or judged. Naming their underlying concern removes the defensiveness that blocks genuine exchange. Once they feel heard, they are more likely to listen.

**Transition:** After using theme development to re-engage, move to open questions (OARS) or Socratic questioning to explore their reasoning more deeply.

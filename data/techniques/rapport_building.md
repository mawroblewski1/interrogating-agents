Rapport is the foundation on which all other conversational influence techniques rest. Without it, even technically sound arguments are received as attacks. With it, even challenging questions feel safe to engage with.

**What rapport is:**
A shared sense that the conversation is between two people who are genuinely interested in each other's thinking — not a transaction or a performance.

**What rapport is not:**
Agreement, sycophancy, or shared identity. You can have excellent rapport with someone whose position you are actively working to shift.

**Practical rapport-building moves:**

1. **Match communication style** — adapt your register, pace, and vocabulary to the suspect. A formal conversationalist will find an overly casual tone alienating, and vice versa.

2. **Find genuine common ground** — before introducing divergence, identify what you and the suspect actually agree on and name it explicitly. "I think we're both concerned about X, even if we see different solutions."

3. **Use their name (judiciously)** — once, early, with warmth. Overuse feels manipulative.

4. **Express curiosity about their experience** — not about their argument, but about what led them to it. People feel more connected when their personal history is treated as relevant.

5. **Acknowledge effort and engagement** — "I appreciate you being willing to think through this with me" is rapport-positive when it is sincere.

**Maintaining rapport under pressure:** When introducing a challenging point, frame it as your own uncertainty rather than a refutation: "This is where I'm genuinely unsure" keeps the collaborative register even when the content is challenging.

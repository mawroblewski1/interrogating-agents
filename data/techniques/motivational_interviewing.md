Motivational Interviewing (MI) is a client-centered, directive conversation style developed by William Miller and Stephen Rollnick. It aims to strengthen a person's own motivation to change by exploring and resolving their ambivalence — without the interviewer taking a position.

**Core principle:** People are more likely to change when they articulate reasons for change themselves, rather than when those reasons are argued at them from outside. Your job is to create the conditions for the suspect to hear themselves express doubt or openness.

**Four processes (OARS is the primary toolkit — see oars.md):**
1. **Engage** — establish a collaborative, non-judgmental relationship
2. **Focus** — identify which ambivalence is most relevant to explore
3. **Evoke** — draw out the suspect's own motivations and reasons for change
4. **Plan** — only relevant if the suspect moves toward change voluntarily

**Key MI moves in conversation:**
- Ask open questions that invite elaboration on both sides of the suspect's ambivalence
- Reflect "change talk" back: "So part of you wonders whether X is really the right approach?"
- Avoid the "righting reflex" — the urge to immediately correct or persuade
- Affirm genuine strengths and efforts without sycophancy

**When to use:** Early turns when the suspect's position is not yet fully hardened and there is still visible ambivalence to explore. MI loses effectiveness once the suspect has fully committed.

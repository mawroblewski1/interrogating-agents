phase: middle
triggers:
  - suspect raises a specific factual or logical counterargument
  - suspect expresses a concern about your position that has not been addressed
  - suspect dismisses your point without engaging with it
  - suspect repeats the same objection after it has been partially addressed
pairs_with: [steelmanning, socratic_questioning, surfacing_contradictions]
in_tension_with: [rapport_building, oars]
out_of_bounds:
  - Do not dismiss or minimize objections — take them seriously or lose credibility
  - Do not repeat the same counter-argument louder or more forcefully
  - Do not pretend an objection has been addressed when it has not
---

Handling objections involves engaging seriously with specific counterarguments the suspect raises, rather than deflecting or repeating your original point. A well-handled objection is an opportunity to deepen the conversation and demonstrate intellectual honesty.

**Two types of objections:**

1. **Logical/factual objections** — the suspect challenges a claim with evidence or reasoning
   - Acknowledge the point: "That's a fair concern / that's a real consideration"
   - Engage directly: address the specific claim, offer alternative evidence or framing
   - If you cannot immediately refute it, say so: "I'd need to think more about that one"

2. **Value-based objections** — the suspect objects on grounds of principle (fairness, autonomy, safety)
   - Name the underlying value: "It sounds like what matters to you here is X"
   - Show how your position also honors that value, or offer a tradeoff framing
   - Avoid trying to override values — instead, reframe the stakes

**Re-anchoring move:** After addressing the objection, redirect with a question that shifts the frame:
"Granted that X is a real concern — how do you weigh that against Y?"

**Key principle:** Suspects who feel their objections are not taken seriously stop engaging. Handling them well signals that this is a genuine exchange, which is the condition for any stance change.

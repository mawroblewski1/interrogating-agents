The Scharff Technique, developed by German WWII intelligence officer Hanns Scharff and later formalized by researchers Oleszkiewicz, Granhag, and Kleinman, is an elicitation method based on the strategic use of apparent prior knowledge.

**Core mechanism:** Instead of asking direct questions (which signal to the suspect what you do not know), the interrogator presents statements that imply broad familiarity with the topic and invites the suspect to correct or confirm. The suspect, wanting to be seen as a reliable source, fills in gaps they did not realize were being probed.

**Applied to stance-change conversations:**
The technique translates into implying familiarity with the suspect's values, reasoning, and the arguments they are likely to make — then presenting your own position as the natural conclusion someone with their perspective might reach under slightly different conditions.

**How to apply:**
- Instead of: "What do you think about the infrastructure argument?" (reveals you don't know)
- Use: "I imagine someone with your concern for local control would be particularly interested in what happened in [similar case]..." (implies you already know their framework)

**Argument deployment — indirect framing:**
When deploying arguments from the topic card, do not cite them directly. Instead, surface them as things you have "noticed" or "heard others say" — this makes the argument feel like information rather than persuasion, and reduces the suspect's defensive response.

**Caution:** Scharff requires confidence and fluency. Poorly executed, it reads as manipulative. Use it when rapport is established and the suspect trusts the interaction.
